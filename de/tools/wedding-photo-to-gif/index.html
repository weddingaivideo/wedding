How to make a wedding photo GIF

A still photo set on a screen is lovely and then it is over. An animated GIF does one extra thing: it moves. Fade from the first shot to the second, hold each picture long enough to read it, and the whole gallery turns into a loop you can send to somebody who was not at the wedding.

  1. Add the photos. Drop a folder into the box, or click it and browse. JPG, PNG and WebP all work. Nothing is uploaded - the files are read straight off your hard drive by the browser.
  2. Put them in order. Drag a photo up or down, or use the arrow buttons. The list on the left is the running order of the animation.
  3. Choose the movement. A fade is the quietest and works for almost every set of wedding photos. A slide feels like a slideshow. A zoom pulls the eye into the middle of the picture.
  4. Set the speed and the size. The speed control sets how long each photo is held. The size control sets the width of the finished GIF in pixels.
  5. Build, watch, download. The preview is the real GIF, encoded in the page, so what you watch is exactly the file you save. The file size appears above it before you download anything.

If you want the stills as well - for printing, for an album, or to shrink a 12 megapixel phone photo before you send it - Compress Wedding Photos handles that, and HEIC to JPG turns iPhone files into something every browser can open.

Picking the right size and speed

Two numbers decide how a GIF feels: the width of the file and how long each photo is held. Get them right and the animation looks intentional; get them wrong and it looks like a slideshow that lost the plot.

  • 200 to 400 milliseconds per photo is roughly 10 frames per second, and it suits a quick sequence built from similar shots - confetti, a dance floor, the ring boxes arriving one after another.
  • 800 to 1000 milliseconds per photo is the slideshow pace, and it is the right choice for the quiet shots: the getting-ready portraits, the ceremony, the first dance. One published guide recommends 100 to 200 ms for fluid motion and 500 to 1000 ms for slideshow-style GIFs.
  • 320 pixels wide is enough for an email signature and keeps the file tiny. 480 pixels is the safe default for a chat app or a social post - one published maker notes that a 480 pixel, 10 frame animation stays small and loads fast. 800 pixels is for a big screen.
  • Keep the finished file under 5 MB. That is the figure a published size guide gives for practical web use, and it is comfortably inside what the messaging apps accept.
  • Keep it short. Two to six seconds is the length most people watch before they stop. A 20 photo set at 900 milliseconds per photo is already 18 seconds, so pick fewer photos for a chat message and keep the full set for a screen.

Why GIF colours look flatter than your photos

A GIF holds only 256 colours. That is the format, not a fault in your photographs: the format has carried that limit since CompuServe introduced it in 1987, and every GIF in every chat app today still works the same way. A wedding photo with smooth skin tones, a soft sky or a gradient veil has to be squeezed into a much smaller range, and the result reads as slightly grainy or slightly flat.

This tool builds one shared palette of 256 colours from all your photos together, which is the honest way to do it. Every frame draws from the same set of colours, so the animation does not flicker between frames. The trade-off is that a very warm picture and a very cold picture in the same set have to agree on a middle ground, and one of them can shift a shade. That is the same limitation every ranking GIF maker publishes, and there is no way around it without changing the format.

Two practical tips. Keep the set to photos shot in the same light - one golden-hour set looks far better than five sets from five different rooms. And if a shot matters more than the others, give it a smaller file by leaving it out of the loop and posting it separately.

Why some frames have bars around them

A GIF cannot change its size in the middle of an animation, so the canvas is fixed by the first photo you add. If your second photo is a different shape - a tall portrait after a wide group shot - it has to be fitted inside the same frame. That is where the bars come from, and it is the single most common question asked of every free GIF maker, because the tool never explains it.

There are only two honest answers, and this tool gives you both. Fit whole photo keeps every pixel and shows the empty space as bars around the picture. Fill the frame covers the empty space by cropping the photo to the shape of the canvas, which loses a strip of the picture but gives a clean, edge-to-edge animation. For a wedding set that mixes a tall portrait with wide shots, filling the frame usually looks better on a phone, where the bars would otherwise be very obvious.

Order helps too. The first photo sets the shape of the whole animation, so put a photo in roughly the same shape as most of your others in first place, and the bars mostly disappear on their own.

Where a wedding GIF works best

A GIF is a good fit for a short, still, looping moment - the entrance, the kiss, the first dance, the confetti. It is a poor fit for a long ceremony, because a GIF has no sound and no playback controls.

  • Chat apps. A short loop is exactly what these are built for, and a 480 pixel file under a couple of megabytes arrives instantly.
  • Social posts. A silent, looping, self-contained file needs no video player and no link - it starts playing where it is posted.
  • A screen or a digital frame at the venue or at home. Here a GIF that plays once is often better than one that loops, because a loop at the wrong moment is the thing people notice.
  • Email signatures and messages to older relatives, where a video file is too heavy and a link is one more thing to click.

If the moment deserves sound - the vows, the speeches, the first dance - a GIF cannot carry it. the wedding invitation video maker and AI wedding video from photos build a film from the same pictures, and the wedding photo slideshow guide covers how to sequence them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make a GIF from wedding photos?

Add your photos, choose how they should move into each other - a fade, a slide or a zoom - set how long each photo stays on screen, then build the GIF and download it. The whole thing runs in your browser, so the pictures are never uploaded and the finished file is on your device in a few seconds.

Are my photos uploaded to a server?

No. Your photos are read, resized and animated by your own browser, and the GIF is encoded there as well. Nothing is sent to a server, no account is needed, and there is no watermark on the file.

Which image formats can I use?

JPG, PNG and WebP work everywhere. HEIC files from an iPhone can be converted first with the HEIC to JPG tool on this site, and camera RAW files need to be exported to JPG before any browser can open them.

How many photos can I put in one GIF?

Up to 20 photos at a time. A published guide for free GIF makers recommends between 3 and 20 images per animation, and more than that makes the file heavy and the waiting longer.

Why do my photos look grainy in the GIF?

A GIF holds only 256 colours, so a wedding photo with smooth skin tones or a soft sky has to be squeezed into a much smaller colour range. Everything in this GIF shares one palette of 256 colours chosen from all your photos together, so pictures with very different colours can shift a shade.

Why do some frames have bars around them?

The GIF canvas takes its shape from your first photo, because a GIF cannot change size in the middle of an animation. A photo with a different shape is either fitted whole, so the empty space shows as bars, or cropped to fill the frame, so the edges of the photo are cut instead. You can switch between the two at any time.

Can I make the GIF loop forever, or play just once?

Both. Loop forever is the default and is what a chat app or a social post expects. Play once writes the file without a loop instruction, so it stops on the last photo - the right choice for a screen or a digital frame.

How do I make the GIF faster or slower?

The speed control sets how long each photo is held, from 200 milliseconds to 2 seconds. For a calm slideshow of wedding portraits use 800 to 1000 milliseconds per photo. For a quick motion sequence use 200 to 400 milliseconds, which is roughly 10 frames per second.

What size should my wedding GIF be?

480 pixels is the safe choice for a chat app or an email, and it is the size a published guide recommends for a small, fast animation. Use 320 pixels for an email signature or a small sticker, and 800 pixels when the file is going on a big screen. Keep the finished file under 5 MB for practical web use.

Is the wedding photo to GIF maker free?

Yes. There is no account, no watermark, no daily limit and no charge. The GIF is encoded and saved on your own device, and every tool on this site works the same way.
