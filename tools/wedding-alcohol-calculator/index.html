The rule behind every wedding bar calculator

Ranked US tools (WedBuild, Calculator City, and the catering guides they cite) all start from the same planning rule: one drink per drinking guest for every hour the bar is open, rising to two or three for a crowd that likes a big night. That number is then split across wine, beer and spirits and converted into purchase units. Our default — 100 drinking guests over five hours — gives 500 drinks, exactly the worked example used on the top-ranking page.

Two details matter more than the arithmetic. First, count drinkers, not invitations: children, drivers and guests who do not drink are excluded, and destination or Sunday-afternoon weddings skew lower. Second, check what the venue already supplies before buying anything — many catering packages include the bar, and some venues forbid outside alcohol.

The pour definitions match the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism's standard-drink guidance, which the leading calculators also cite. Switch to the AU standard if you are buying in Australia: 375 ml cans and 700 ml spirit bottles change the case math noticeably.

Worked example: 100 guests, five hours

At one drink per hour with a balanced 50/30/20 split, the shopping list lands near 50 bottles of wine, 7 cases of beer and 5 bottles of spirits, plus about 20 bottles of sparkling wine if every guest gets a toast pour. Add 10% and you will not run short; unopened cases can usually be returned, so erring high costs little.

Planning the rest of the reception

Alcohol is only one line in the catering conversation. Pair this with the cake serving calculator for dessert portions, the guest count calculator to firm up the headcount before RSVPs arrive, and the budget calculator to see how the bar sits against the total. These are estimates for purchasing, not guidance about consumption — offer food, water and good non-alcoholic options, and make sure everyone has a safe way home.

Frequently asked questions

How much alcohol do I need for 100 wedding guests?

At one drink per guest per hour over a five-hour reception, plan for 500 drinks. With a balanced 50% wine / 30% beer / 20% spirits split that is about 50 bottles of wine, 7 cases of beer and 5 bottles of spirits, plus roughly 20 bottles of sparkling wine if everyone gets a toast pour.

How many drinks per guest per hour should I plan for?

One per hour is the standard planning rule. Use 0.8 for a daytime or light-drinking crowd, 1.2 for a lively evening, and 1.5 for a big party. The figure is a purchasing estimate, not a target for anyone's consumption.

Should I count every invited guest?

No. Count the guests who will actually drink — exclude children, designated drivers and non-drinkers. US RSVP guidance also suggests 80–85% of local invitees attend, and fewer for destination weddings, so start from a realistic headcount.

What is a standard drink pour?

In the US: 5 oz of wine, 12 oz of beer, or 1.5 oz of spirits. A 750 ml wine bottle yields about 5 glasses, a case holds 24 beers, and a 750 ml spirit bottle yields about 16 shots. The calculator uses these conversions and always rounds up.

Do I need sparkling wine for the toast?

Only if you are serving a toast pour to every guest. One 5 oz glass each means one 750 ml bottle per five drinking guests. Many venues pour the toast from the regular wine order instead, so check the catering package first.

Can I bring my own alcohol to the venue?

Sometimes. Many US venues allow self-supplied alcohol but charge a corkage fee, while others require their licensed bar package. Confirm the policy in writing before buying — this calculator works out the gap you need to cover.
