The question every US wedding guest googles

The Knot's guest study puts the average US wedding gift at about $150, with roughly half of guests giving a physical gift, 40% cash and 10% a gift card. Etiquette pages that rank for this query publish near-identical bands by relationship — which is what the calculator above uses as its starting point.

RelationshipWidely cited range
Coworker, neighbour, acquaintance$50–$75 (up to $150 in some guides)
Friend or extended family$75–$125

Immediate family / wedding party$200–$300

What actually changes the number

Ranked calculators (ToolAspect, MoneyDeck, Wedding Card Boxes) apply the same handful of adjustments: a plus-one adds roughly $50 because the couple is feeding two of you; a destination wedding lowers the range by about $40 because your flights and hotel are already part of your generosity; not attending cuts it by roughly 30%; and gifts already given at a shower count toward the total. Your budget is the only figure that really matters — no etiquette guide says anyone should go into debt over a gift.

The "cover your plate" rule is obsolete

The old rule said your gift should equal the cost of your meal. Most modern etiquette writers now reject it: a gift is a token of celebration, not a transaction. It is still useful as a sanity check — The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study puts the average US wedding at about $34,200 for 117 guests, roughly $292 per guest all-in, with catering around $80 a head — but no one is auditing your envelope.

Cash, cheque, registry or fund?

Cash is simplest for the couple; a cheque is safer if the card box is unattended; gift cards and honeymoon funds suit couples who have said what they want. Put the gift in a card with both your names and leave it at the card box rather than handing it to a busy couple. Frequently asked questions

How much should I give as a wedding gift in 2026?

The Knot's guest study puts the US average at about $150. Widely cited bands run $50–$75 for a coworker, $75–$125 for a friend, $125–$200 for a close friend or relative, and $200–$300 for immediate family or the wedding party.

Is the cover-your-plate rule still a thing?

Most etiquette writers now consider it obsolete. A gift is a celebration, not payment for a meal. It can still be a useful floor — catering averages around $80 a head — but your budget and your relationship matter more.

Do I give more if I bring a plus-one?

Usually a single joint gift, larger than a solo gift but not double. Ranked calculators add roughly $50 to each end of the range for a plus-one, since the couple is hosting two people.

Should I give less for a destination wedding?

Yes, and couples expect it. Etiquette guidance broadly agrees that heavy travel and hotel costs count toward your generosity; the calculator reduces the range accordingly.

Do I have to give a gift if I cannot attend?

Not always. A card alone is acceptable for a distant acquaintance, and many guides suggest scaling a gift down by about 30% if you do send one. Close family and friends usually still send something.

Should I give cash or a physical gift?

Both are normal. In The Knot's guest study about half gave a physical gift, 40% cash and 10% a gift card. Follow the registry or honeymoon fund if the couple has shared one.
